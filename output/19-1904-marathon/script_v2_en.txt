[CHAPTER 1 - A RACE BUILT ON SHORTCUTS]
St. Louis, Missouri. August 30th, 1904.
The Olympics were being held alongside a World's Fair, and honestly, the fair got more attention.
The marathon started in the afternoon.
It was over ninety degrees.
The route ran along dirt roads, up seven hills, and through clouds of dust kicked up by officials' cars driving ahead of the runners.
And the organizers had set up exactly one water stop.
A well. About twelve miles in.
By many accounts, the man in charge, James Sullivan, wanted to see what happened to runners without much water.
That was the first shortcut. It wasn't taken by a runner.
Thirty-two men started.
Within hours, one of them, William Garcia, was found collapsed by the road. The dust had torn up his insides. He nearly died.
And this was just the warm-up.

[CHAPTER 2 - THE MAN IN THE CAR]
One of the favorites was a New Yorker named Fred Lorz.
Around mile nine, the heat beat him.
He stopped. He was done.
His manager pulled up in a car, and Lorz climbed in.
Fair enough. He quit.
Except the car drove on along the course.
For about eleven miles, Lorz rode past the runners who were still suffering in the dust.
Then the car broke down.
And Lorz, now well rested, got out... and started jogging again.
He jogged all the way into the stadium.
He crossed the finish line first.
The crowd went wild. He posed for photos. By some accounts, he was about to be handed the prize by the President's own daughter.
Then people who had seen him on the road spoke up.
Lorz admitted it. He said it was a joke.
Officials didn't laugh. He was banned for life.
So if the man who crossed the line first didn't win...
who did?

[CHAPTER 3 - APPLES, NAPS AND A DOG]
While all this was happening, the rest of the race was falling apart in its own way.
A Cuban mailman named Andarín Carvajal had reportedly lost his money gambling on the way to St. Louis.
He showed up in long trousers, so someone cut them into shorts right at the starting line.
During the race, he chatted with spectators.
He stopped at an orchard and ate some apples.
They were rotten.
He got stomach cramps, lay down by the road, and took a nap.
Then he got up and finished fourth.
Two runners, Len Tau and Jan Mashiani, were Tswana men from southern Africa who happened to be in St. Louis for the fair.
They became the first Africans to run in the Olympics.
Tau was chased about a mile off the course by an angry dog. He still finished ninth.
Out of thirty-two men, only fourteen finished at all.
So far, this sounds like a comedy.
The winner's story is where it stops being funny.

[CHAPTER 4 - THE WINNER ON POISON]
Thomas Hicks was an English-born runner competing for the United States.
By mile eighteen, he was falling apart. He begged to lie down.
His trainers said no.
Instead, they gave him a milligram of strychnine — a stimulant in small doses, and a rat poison in larger ones — mixed with raw egg whites.
Then a sip of brandy.
It wasn't against the rules. There were no rules about it.
Later, they gave him another dose.
In the final miles, Hicks reportedly saw things that weren't there.
He walked. He staggered. His trainers held him up.
He crossed the line in three hours, twenty-eight minutes and fifty-three seconds, one of the slowest winning times in Olympic history.
He won gold.
He had won the Olympics. And he could barely stand.
