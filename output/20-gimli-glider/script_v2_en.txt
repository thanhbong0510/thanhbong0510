[HOOK]
Have you ever messed up a unit?
Cups instead of grams. Fahrenheit instead of Celsius.
Your cake comes out flat. You laugh. You order pizza.
Now imagine the recipe is for a plane.
And the cake is sixty-nine people, seven miles in the sky.
In 1983, a brand-new Boeing 767 ran out of fuel at forty-one thousand feet.
Not because of a leak.
Because of one small number, written in the wrong unit.

[MYTH + PROMISE]
We like to think disasters need a villain.
Someone careless. Someone lazy. One big, stupid mistake.
This one didn't have a villain.
It had a dozen careful people, each doing their job, each trusting the number in front of them.
And what saved everyone wasn't in any manual.
It was what two pilots happened to do with their lives outside of work.
One of those things involved a racetrack full of families.

[CHAPTER 1 - THE NEW PLANE]
July 23rd, 1983. Air Canada Flight 143, Montreal to Edmonton.
The plane was a Boeing 767, only a few months old. The latest thing.
It had glass screens instead of old dials, and it was the first plane in Air Canada's fleet to measure fuel in kilograms.
Canada was switching to the metric system.
The rest of the fleet still used pounds.
That alone wasn't a problem.
But this plane had a second one.
Its fuel gauges weren't working.
A faulty part meant the computer couldn't show how much fuel was in the tanks.
There was a way around it. The crew could measure the tanks by hand, with a dipstick, like checking the oil in your car, and then do the math.
So that's what they did.
Remember that math. It matters.

[CHAPTER 2 - THE NUMBER]
The fuel was measured in litres.
To get the weight, the crew multiplied by 1.77.
That's the number for pounds per litre.
But the plane needed kilograms.
The right number was about 0.8.
Nobody noticed.
Not the fuelers. Not the mechanics. Not the pilots, who checked the math together.
Everyone had done this calculation a thousand times, in pounds.
The result looked like a perfectly normal number.
The plane needed 22,300 kilograms of fuel to make the trip.
It had about 22,300 pounds.
Less than half.
They even stopped in Ottawa on the way, measured again, and made the exact same mistake again.
Then they took off for Edmonton.
The flight computer, fed that number, said they had plenty of fuel.
And then a warning light came on.

[CHAPTER 3 - SILENCE AT 41,000 FEET]
Over northern Ontario, the cockpit beeped: low fuel pressure, left side.
The pilots thought it was a broken pump.
Then the right side beeped too.
They turned toward Winnipeg.
The left engine stopped.
Then, a few minutes later, the right engine stopped.
The cockpit filled with a long bong sound that neither pilot had ever heard before.
Nobody had heard it. It was the alarm for "all engines off," and nobody had expected it to ever be used.
Then the modern glass screens went dark.
They ran on power from the engines.
The pilots were left with a few small backup instruments, the kind you'd find in a much older plane.
A small propeller dropped out of the plane's belly to give them just enough power to steer.
Sixty-one passengers and eight crew were now sitting in a giant glider.
And the 767 manual had no chapter for gliding.

[CHAPTER 4 - THE HOBBY AND THE MEMORY]
Here's where the luck starts.
Captain Bob Pearson wasn't just an airline pilot.
On weekends, he flew gliders. Planes with no engines at all.
He knew how to read the air. How to stretch a glide.
First Officer Maurice Quintal did the math on their height and distance.
They weren't going to make Winnipeg.
But Quintal remembered something.
Years earlier, in the Air Force, he had been based at a small airfield nearby. A place called Gimli.
What he didn't know was that Gimli's old runway had been turned into a racetrack.
And that day, it was family day.
Cars. Campers. By many accounts, kids on bicycles near the runway.
The plane came in too high and too fast.
So Pearson did something you're not supposed to do in an airliner.
He turned the controls in opposite directions and flew the jet sideways, a glider pilot's trick, to dump height fast.
The passengers looked out their windows at the ground, tilted at a strange angle.
Then the landing gear dropped. The nose wheel didn't lock.

[CHAPTER 5 - THE LANDING]
The 767 hit the runway.
Pearson stood on the brakes so hard that tires blew out.
The nose wheel folded, and the plane's nose scraped along the concrete, throwing sparks.
That scraping slowed it down even faster.
It stopped, by many accounts, a few hundred feet from the people on the track.
A small fire started in the nose.
People from the race day ran over with handheld fire extinguishers and helped put it out.
Everyone got off. A few people were hurt on the emergency slides. Nobody died.
And afterwards?
At first, the pilots were blamed. Pearson was demoted for six months. Quintal was suspended for two weeks.
Later, the investigation pointed at the system instead: the confusing switch to metric, the broken gauge, the unclear rules about who checks the fuel.
The two pilots received an international award for outstanding airmanship.
And the plane? It was repaired and flew for another twenty-five years.
People called it the Gimli Glider.

[ENDING]
So think back to that ruined cake.
The wrong unit. The flat sponge. The pizza.
In 1983, the same kind of mistake was made by careful people who checked their work.
It wasn't caught by any rule or any computer.
It was saved by a man who spent his weekends flying planes with no engines,
and another who remembered an old runway from a job years before.
Sometimes the most important skill you have isn't the one on your résumé.
So here's my question:
who should take the blame for Flight 143 — the people who did the math, or the system that let one number go unchecked?
